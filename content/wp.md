### Working Papers

**Paired Movers: A New Test of (log) Additive Separability**  (with [Isaac Sorkin](https://sites.google.com/site/isaacsorkin/home?)) -- [PDF](https://github.com/lucaswarwar/lucaswarwar.github.io/main/content/sw_log_additivity.pdf)
<details>
  <summary>[Abstract]</summary>
  Consider a pair of workers who both move from firm A to firm B. In a model with additively separable firm and worker effects, the pay gap between them at the origin firm should perfectly predict the gap at the destination firm (a forecast coefficient of 1). Using data from Brazil and Italy, we find forecast coefficients of 0.70 and 0.82, rejecting additive separability and indicating the presence of match effects. Under exogenous mobility, these estimates imply that the variance of match effects is about 40% of the variance of firm effects. In a model with endogenous mobility, these estimates imply a larger role for match effects. These estimates imply a larger role for match effects than previously thought. In simulated data, we show that standard specification tests are often biased or underpowered, and that extrapolating earnings patterns from observed to unobserved job offers can be misleading when match effects are important.
</details>

**Intergenerational Mobility in the Land of Inequality** (with D. Britto, A. Fonseca, P. Pinotti, and B. Sampaio) -- [PDF](https://github.com/lucaswarwar/lucaswarwar.github.io/main/content/IGM_BFPSW.pdf) -- *R&R, REStat*
<details>
  <summary>[Abstract]</summary>
  We provide the first estimates of intergenerational income mobility using population-wide tax data for a large developing country, namely Brazil. We measure formal income from tax and payroll data, and we train machine learning models on census and survey data to predict informal income. We quantify the estimation bias resulting from income imputation and other sources of measurement error, and show that such bias remains negligible in our context. A 10 percentile increase in parental income rank is associated on average with a 5.5 percentile increase in child income rank, with considerable variation across sociodemographic groups and geographical areas.
</details>
